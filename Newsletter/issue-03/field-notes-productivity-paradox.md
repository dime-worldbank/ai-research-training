# Field Notes: The Productivity Paradox and the J-Curve

*Background article for AI for Research, Issue 3. Reading time: about 8 minutes.*

This issue's Big Number comes from firm surveys: executives at nearly **750** firms report AI-related productivity gains that exceed what their revenue and employment figures imply ([Baslandze et al., NBER WP 34984, 2026](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34984)). Perceived gains run ahead of measured gains. For teams templating decks and briefs, that is also a scheduling note: expect the first automated output to feel slower than the old habit.

## What the executive survey found

Baslandze and coauthors surveyed senior decision-makers about AI investment, productivity, and workforce plans.

Three findings matter for how we read "AI is making us faster":

1. **Perceived > measured.** CFO-reported labor-productivity improvements from AI are larger than the revenue-based gains implied by reported changes in revenue and employment. The authors mainly attribute the gap to delayed output realization and quality improvements not yet visible in revenues.
2. **Measured gains are positive and expected to grow.** Implied revenue-based labor-productivity effects were already positive in 2025 and are expected to roughly double in 2026. The largest effects are in high-skill services and finance (about 0.8% implied LP growth in 2025 in those sectors; expected 2026 gains above 2% in the same group).
3. **Organization matters more than capital deepening in the near term.** At the typical firm, only a small share of near-term LP gains comes from higher capital per worker. Most of the residual is revenue-based TFP: efficiency, product quality, intermediates, markups. How work is organized shows up more than how many AI licenses were bought.

The tools can help before the spreadsheet fully reflects that help, especially while firms are still changing processes.

## The J-curve: why process work slows you down at first

The [ICLE evidence review (2026)](https://laweconcenter.org/resources/ai-productivity-and-labor-markets-a-review-of-the-empirical-evidence/) puts this in the Brynjolfsson–Rock–Syverson **productivity J-curve** frame. New general-purpose tools often need training, templates, new review habits, and software glue. Those costs can hold down measured productivity for a while before gains show up.

On a research team:

| Phase | What it looks like | How it feels |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Setup** | Writing a prepare-slides outline gate; freezing a Quarto brief template; encoding "do not invent numbers" | Slower than copy-paste into last quarter's deck |
| **Refill** | Second and third pass from updated `outputs/tables/` | Faster; fewer transcription errors |

Task-level experiments often show large gains on single tasks. Firm- and department-level series move later, once the setup work exists. Both can be true at once.

## What this means for Issue 3

Issue 1's Field Notes used METR to warn against trusting feelings of speed. Issue 3's point is related but narrower: even when the tools help, department-level output can lag while you build the refills.

| Temptation | Better move |
| --- | --- |
| Judge prepare-slides by the first deck only | Judge it by deck #2 and #3 from the same results folder |
| Ask a model to write the brief from a PDF | Freeze slots; fill from a schema; check every number |
| Drop templating because week one slipped | Treat week one as setup time the J-curve predicts |

The July 30 skill already uses propose, then approve, then build. That gate is setup work. Skipping it to save time often means you get the lag without the later refill gains.

## What to do this week

- **Budget the first pass.** Block time for Phase 1 (slides) or template plus schema (briefs). Do not schedule the first automated memo as if it were a refill.
- **Time the refill.** Track minutes from "tables updated" to "deck or brief compiled," not how the chat felt.
- **Keep the surrounding pieces.** House rules (Issues 1 and 2), the prepare-slides skill, and a checked matrix-to-document pipe (this issue's Deep Dive) are what make refills cheap.
- **Keep claims with the author.** Automation can refill structure and paste checked numbers. Policy conclusions still need the author's sign-off.

## One number to remember

Remember the lag: perceived usefulness often arrives before measured output, in firms and on research teams. Plan for setup time. Count gains on the refill.
